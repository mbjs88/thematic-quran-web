Aṭ-Ṭāriq — The Night-Comer

Wa-s-samāʾi wa-ṭ-ṭāriq
Meccan· 17 verses· 6 sections· Juzʾ 30
About this reading This overview is compiled from many classical and modern commentaries, in several languages, gathered into one plain-English reading. It reflects only what those sources say, not independent opinion. The small numbers, like 1,3, point to the commentaries a statement draws on; you can find them listed at the end. Where the scholars read a verse differently, both readings are given rather than settled.
Section 1 · verses 1–3

An oath by the night sky

In short God swears by the sky and by a bright star that appears at night. The oath is building toward a promise about us, in the verse that follows: nothing we do goes unwatched.

1وَالسَّمَاءِ وَالطَّارِقِBy the sky and the night-comer—
2وَمَا أَدْرَاكَ مَا الطَّارِقُWhat will explain to you what the night-comer is?
3النَّجْمُ الثَّاقِبُThe piercing star—

The "night-comer" is a star. The word means anything that arrives by night, and a star earns the name because it shows itself only after dark.1,2,3,5

Which star? The commentators leave it open. Most take it to mean bright stars in general;1,3,7 some name a particular one, such as Saturn or the Pleiades.3,5 What they agree on is the quality the third verse adds: "the piercing star," one so bright it seems to bore through the darkness with its light.1,3,4,7

The question in between, "what will explain to you what the night-comer is?", is the Qurʾan's way of lifting up the thing it swears by, holding our attention a moment before it answers.3,4,6

The sky and its brightest night-light are called as witnesses. The oath is deliberately grand, because what it is about to affirm is meant to be taken with the same weight.

A modern lensevocative resonance

The Qurʾan is taken here as fixed, revealed truth; science is our provisional, evolving account of the observable world. What follows notes where the two meet — a contemplation, not a proof. Where the science later shifts, that reflects the limits of our knowledge, not the text.

The commentators read ṭāriq as a star that comes by night, and al-Qurṭubī notes its root also carries the sense of knocking or striking, the same root that gives the word for a hammer. "The piercing star" is one whose light bores through the dark. A modern ear, listening with today's astronomy, may catch pulsars in it: rotating neutron stars, first detected in 1967, that beat out radio pulses across space at astonishingly regular intervals, like a steady knocking. That hearing belongs to our moment and may well change, since pulsars are found by radio telescope, not the naked eye, and the plain meaning remains simply a bright night star. The verse stands either way. A resonance to sit with, not a claim to defend.

Sources: Pulsars and the discovery of neutron stars · Pulsar overview

Section 2 · verses 4–7

Someone is watching; look at where you began

In short Here is what the oath was for: over every single person there is a watcher, recording what they do. Then the surah turns us to a humbling fact, our own beginning, to make a larger point believable.

4إِن كُلُّ نَفْسٍ لَّمَّا عَلَيْهَا حَافِظٌthere is a watcher over every soul.
5فَلْيَنظُرِ الْإِنسَانُ مِمَّ خُلِقَMan should reflect on what he was created from.
6خُلِقَ مِن مَّاءٍ دَافِقٍHe is created from spurting fluid,
7يَخْرُجُ مِن بَيْنِ الصُّلْبِ وَالتَّرَائِبِthen he emerges from between the backbone and breastbone:

"There is a watcher over every soul." The commentators explain this as angels who record each person's deeds, good and bad, to be accounted for later;1,2,5 some add that they also guard a person from harm until the appointed time.3,5 Whether the watcher is understood as these angels or, behind them, as God Himself, the meaning holds: nothing is unseen.4,6 One reflective commentary reads the bright night-star of the opening as itself a reminder of this Watcher, recording what we do.12

Then comes the turn: "man should reflect on what he was created from." Sending us back to our beginning has a purpose, to make the next life believable, since the God who first shaped a person from almost nothing can certainly restore him.1,2,10

The description is deliberately humbling: created from a fluid that spurts, emerging "from between the backbone and the breastbone."2,8 Many read this as the man's backbone and the woman's chest;2,5,8,10 others take it as describing where the fluid issues from in the body.7 Al-Ṭabarī records a wide range of views on the exact place and settles on "the place of the necklace" on the chest.1 Some modern commentaries pause here to connect the verse with what is now understood about how human life begins.10,11

The message underneath is simple and steadying. You are watched, and you began as something small, and the One who made you from so little is the One you will answer to.

A modern lensstrong resonance

The Qurʾan is taken here as fixed, revealed truth; science is our provisional, evolving account of the observable world. What follows notes where the two meet — a contemplation, not a proof. Where the science later shifts, that reflects the limits of our knowledge, not the text.

The verse says the reproductive fluid issues "from between the backbone and the ribs." What we can observe today is that the organs producing it, the testes, do not begin in the scrotum at all: in the embryo they form high in the abdomen, alongside the kidneys, in the region between the spine and the lower ribs, and only later descend. A lasting trace of that origin stays for life, as their blood vessels and nerves still reach down from that high, mid-back level. The words were settled fourteen centuries ago; our anatomy is the latecomer arriving at the same place. Several commentators already reached toward this (al-Bayḍāwī, cited in Maʿārif; Ibn ʿĀshūr and Sayyid Quṭb, in al-Wasīṭ). Held as wonder, beside the plain meaning, not in place of it.

Sources: Development of the gonads · Embryology, Testicle (StatPearls)

Section 3 · verses 8–10

He can bring you back

In short The God who made you can remake you. On the Day your hidden self is laid bare, nothing in you or around you will be able to shield you.

8إِنَّهُ عَلَىٰ رَجْعِهِ لَقَادِرٌGod is certainly able to bring him back to life.
9يَوْمَ تُبْلَى السَّرَائِرُOn the Day when secrets are laid bare
10فَمَا لَهُ مِن قُوَّةٍ وَلَا نَاصِرٍhe will have no power and no one to help him.

"God is certainly able to bring him back to life." Having pointed to how a person begins, the surah draws the natural conclusion: the same power can return him after death.1,2,7 A few commentators read "bring him back" in other senses, such as returning a person from old age toward youth, but the reading most take, and which al-Ṭabarī and others prefer, is resurrection, because the next verse speaks of the Day of Judgement.1,3,5,7

"On the Day when secrets are laid bare" — everything a person kept hidden, their beliefs, their intentions, and the deeds they did in private, is brought into the open and tested.1,7,9 Some commentators note that the acts most easily hidden, like prayer, fasting, and washing, are exactly the "secrets" that will be examined, since a person could always have claimed to do them.1,3,5

And on that Day, "he will have no power and no one to help him." No strength of his own, and no ally or supporter, can shield him.1,2,3 In this life a person leans on family and allies; there, that support is gone.1,3

The passage closes the circle it opened. The One who watches you and made you will raise you, on a day when the inside of every life is turned out, and nothing stands between a person and their account.

Section 4 · verses 11–14

A word to be taken seriously

In short God swears again, this time by the returning rain and the splitting earth, to affirm one thing: the Qurʾan is a decisive word that sorts truth from falsehood. It is not a joke.

11وَالسَّمَاءِ ذَاتِ الرَّجْعِBy the sky and its recurring rain,
12وَالْأَرْضِ ذَاتِ الصَّدْعِby the earth that cracks open!
13إِنَّهُ لَقَوْلٌ فَصْلٌThis is truly a decisive statement;
14وَمَا هُوَ بِالْهَزْلِit is not something to be taken lightly.

The surah swears by "the sky and its recurring rain" and "the earth that cracks open." The rain returns year after year;1,5,9 the earth splits to let plants push through.1,2,5 Between them they carry the whole cycle of life, and, the commentators note, they quietly echo the surah's theme: the God who revives the dead earth with rain can revive the dead.7,10

What the oath affirms is the Qurʾan: "this is truly a decisive statement." It separates truth from falsehood and settles what people dispute.1,3,7,8 Early commentators gloss "decisive" as true, or as a binding judgement.1,2,3

"It is not something to be taken lightly." It is serious through and through, not play or idle talk.1,5,7 One commentary draws out what that should mean for the reader: a word God Himself calls decisive deserves to be met with weight and awe, not treated casually.4

Rain and greening earth stand as witnesses, and the point they underwrite is plain: this is a word that decides, and it asks to be taken seriously.

A modern lenslight resonance

The Qurʾan is taken here as fixed, revealed truth; science is our provisional, evolving account of the observable world. What follows notes where the two meet — a contemplation, not a proof. Where the science later shifts, that reflects the limits of our knowledge, not the text.

Here the classical reading already is, in effect, the science: the rain that returns season after season, and the earth that cracks open for growing plants. A modern eye simply recognises the water cycle and seed germination in the same words. This verse hides no fact so much as point steadily at a cycle we now describe in finer detail, and the point it was making, that the God who returns rain to dead ground can return the dead to life, is untouched by any of that detail.

Section 5 · verses 15–16

They scheme, and God answers

In short The deniers work quietly against the truth. God, in answer, has a plan of His own, and theirs cannot outmatch it.

15إِنَّهُمْ يَكِيدُونَ كَيْدًاThey plot and scheme,
16وَأَكِيدُ كَيْدًاbut so do I:

"They plot and scheme." The Meccan opponents worked in secret against the Prophet and his message, trying to defeat the truth and prop up falsehood.1,5,7,9

"But so do I." The commentators describe God's answer not as trickery but as a wise, measured response: letting the deniers run on gradually, from where they do not expect it, until He takes them at the time He chooses.3,4,5 Saʿdī adds the reassurance underneath it: a human being is far too weak to out-plan the All-Powerful, the All-Knowing.7

The scene is set as a contest that is no contest. Their scheming is real, but it runs up against a plan it cannot see and cannot beat.

Section 6 · verse 17

Give them a little time

In short The surah ends by calming the Prophet: do not rush for their punishment. Leave them be, just for a little while.

17فَمَهِّلِ الْكَافِرِينَ أَمْهِلْهُمْ رُوَيْدًا[Prophet], let the disbelievers be, let them be for a while.

"Let the disbelievers be, let them be for a while." The Prophet is told not to hasten their downfall, but to give them a short respite.1,3,8 "A while" is glossed as a little, a short time.1,3,7 The gentle repetition, "let them be… let them be," deepens the call to be patient.4,8

Classical commentators note that this instruction to hold back was later followed, once permission to respond had come, by the command to fight, and they point to what befell the Meccan leaders soon afterwards.3,5,8

The ending is quietly confident. The outcome is settled; only the timing is left open, and the Prophet is asked simply to wait.

The commentaries

The numbers above point to these sources. Each has its own strength, which is why they are read together.

  1. al-Ṭabarī, Jāmiʿ al-Bayān — the foundational early commentary; gathers the range of early opinion.
  2. Ibn Kathīr — explains verses through the Qurʾan and prophetic reports.
  3. al-Qurṭubī, al-Jāmiʿ li-Aḥkām al-Qurʾan — strong on legal points, context, and language.
  4. al-Zamakhsharī, al-Kashshāf — close attention to the language and rhetoric.
  5. al-Baghawī, Maʿālim al-Tanzīl — careful early reports and consensus readings.
  6. al-Biqāʿī, Naẓm al-Durar — how each verse fits the surah's overall purpose.
  7. al-Saʿdī, Taysīr al-Karīm al-Raḥmān — the practical lesson to take from each passage.
  8. al-Jalālayn — a short, word-by-word explanation.
  9. al-Muyassar — a plain, mainstream summary meaning.
  10. al-Wasīṭ, Muḥammad Sayyid Ṭanṭāwī — a modern reading connected to everyday life.
  11. Maʿārif al-Qurʾan, Muḥammad Shafīʿ — a modern, English-available commentary with practical notes.
  12. Tazkīr al-Qurʾan, Wahiduddin Khan — modern, reflective commentary.
